FAQ

What skaters is for, how to use it, where it stops, and the technical questions that come up most. The conversation primer on the home page gives a chat assistant the same starting point.

About skaters

Why was it written?

To monitor hundreds of thousands of public and private economic series, each with a full predictive distribution, updated online as each observation arrives, with no training job to run and nothing to tune. Nothing small, fast and light did that at that scale, so laplace was built for it.

Why is it called skaters?

The project is older than this package. It began as timemachines, where every forecaster had the signature f(y, s, k, a, t, e, r): the observation y, then state, steps ahead, variables known in advance, time, expiry and hyper-parameters. The signature has since shrunk to f(y, state), and the name stayed.

What does it produce?

A predictive distribution for the next value of one series: a mean, a spread, quantiles and a density. Every call takes one observation and the previous state and returns the forecast and the new state, in O(1) time per observation. The state also carries a calibrated surprise, state["z"] and state["pit"], for each arriving point.

What are laplace and granger?

laplace is the general forecaster: a likelihood-weighted ensemble of transform chains with a distributional leaf. granger is laplace with its continuous hyperparameters trained per frequency (monthly, weekly, hourly), mixed with laplace so that its total log score is never more than log 4 behind. The methodology guide describes both.

Using it

How do I start?

pip install skaters, then feed observations one at a time to laplace(). The examples page has runnable recipes: a first forecast, several steps ahead, positive series, calibration checks, anomalies, mean reversion, your own transform and the browser.

Which languages?

Python, with a Rust core for speed, and parity-locked ports in JavaScript, R and Julia that agree with the Python to 1e-6. See languages.

Can an AI assistant help me use it?

Yes. The Claude skills are copyable instructions for common jobs: basic use, benchmarking a method against laplace, reviewing a paper's benchmark, exogenous variables, residual review and others. For a general conversation, paste the primer into any chat assistant.

What is a skater?

Any function f(y, state) -> (distributions, state). It takes one observation and the state it returned last time, and gives back a predictive distribution for each horizon and a new state. laplace, granger and every building block in the package share that signature, so they compose.

How fast is it?

One update costs about 12 microseconds in the Rust core and about 0.3 milliseconds in pure Python, one step ahead on one CPU thread. A pretrained model reruns its forward pass for each new observation, which costs milliseconds to hundreds of milliseconds per forecast. The skaters measurements are on the performance page. The cost of each foundational model per forecast is on the speed page of foundationalprediction.com.

How do I get intervals and quantiles?

From the distribution: d.quantile(0.05) and d.quantile(0.95) give a 90% interval, d.cdf(x) a probability, d.logpdf(y) a log score. On the canonical panel the central 90% interval contains between 89% and 92% of outcomes, depending on the stratum.

What about missing values?

Pass NaN for a missing observation. The forecaster carries its state forward without updating on it, and the calibration diagnostics skip it. Irregularly spaced observations are not modelled as such: the forecaster sees a sequence, and the step is whatever interval separates the values you feed it.

Can I use it with a model I already have?

Yes. Forecast your model's residuals with laplace (the sandwich pattern), run it alongside your model as a calibration check (the sidecar pattern), or combine the two predictive densities with weights that follow realised log score. The combination keeps most of the other model's gains and costs little where the other model is worse.

Can I use it to whiten a series?

Yes, and this is one of its main uses. After each observation the state holds state["z"], the normal quantile of the observation's probability under the forecast issued one step earlier. If the forecast is calibrated, that stream is approximately independent N(0,1). It is causal, because each value uses only the past. The standardization-review skill has a ten-line whiten generator, and the Rosenblatt demo shows the transform running.

How does that differ from a rolling z-score?

A rolling z-score subtracts a trailing mean and divides by a trailing standard deviation. It changes location and scale only, so skew, heavy tails and serial dependence pass through. Labelling |z| > 3 as a three-sigma event then assumes a normality the data may not have. The probability-integral version maps each point through the whole predictive distribution, so shape is handled as well. Neither guarantees calibration. Check the z stream before you attach nominal probabilities to thresholds.

Is the whitened stream really white?

Only as far as the forecaster is right. What it fails to model stays in z as remaining autocorrelation, a scale that drifts or a lopsided histogram. Testing z for those is the calibration check. ARIMA, ETS, GARCH and Prophet fitted to z find only a few hundredths of a nat (results log). On FRED change series the implied sd of z is about 0.88 one step ahead, so the intervals run slightly wide; the homogenization page measures this by horizon and corrects it online.

Can I use it for anomaly detection?

The state carries a calibrated surprise for every arriving point, state["z"], which is the raw material. Turning it into an alarm with a measured false-alarm rate is the job of timemachines, which is built on skaters.

Scope

Why economic series?

Because that is what it was built for and where it has been tested hardest: daily, weekly and monthly economic series, mostly from FRED. On price and return series a fitted GARCH-t is the recommendation, and laplace ties it. On strongly seasonal hourly waveforms some foundation models and Conformal Seasonal Pools are ahead.

What is it not best for?

This can be answered in part by looking at the metrics and the breakdowns by type of series on foundationalprediction.com. Intuitively, a model built for economic time series needs to spread probability over a lot of the space, and it seldom has strong anchors. So, for example, you would probably not want to use it out of the box to predict hourly Citi Bike availability. You can try, of course. If you do, please file a GitHub issue with the results.

Is it a replacement for foundational time-series models?

No. Several pretrained models beat laplace on monthly, weekly and hourly series by a few hundredths of a nat per step; laplace is ahead on daily economic series and is far cheaper and easier to run online. The paired results for each model, against laplace or any other reference, are on foundationalprediction.com, and the protocol is in A stronger univariate benchmark for foundational time-series model evaluation.

What about the classics, like AutoARIMA and GARCH?

Originally skaters was built to be much lighter, and dependency-free for the browser. It turned out to be statistically more suitable too, and much faster, than alternatives such as refitting ARIMA, at least for the intended purpose. If you know the series is very close to a martingale (say, a liquidly traded security), then a GARCH-t is a better bet than laplace. To date, attempts to get laplace to recognise this and switch over have failed, because they cost too much regret on other series. If you have a clever idea for this, file an issue.

Does it handle covariates or exogenous regressors?

No. laplace is univariate: it forecasts the stream it is given and nothing else. It has no way to use known future covariates, calendar regressors or other series. For those, use a foundation model that takes covariates, or put laplace's forecast features into a streaming regression with ice-skaters; the exogenous-variables skill sets out both routes and how to check that the covariates helped.

Is there a multivariate version?

Not in skaters itself: one stream, one forecaster, one distribution. Cross-stream work happens one layer up, where each stream is reduced to its forecaster's expectation and surprise. The first packaged version is ice-skaters, which is preliminary.

Does it work on counts and series with many zeros?

It runs, and the lattice projection gives exact repeated values their own mass, which helps on series that sit on a grid. It has no model for a point mass at zero. For a positive series with zeros, set the floor of flesaker a little below zero, or treat the zeros separately.

How not to use

Forecasting a positive series on the real line without a transform, feeding values near 1020 or 10-12 instead of natural units, asking for horizons far beyond twelve steps, and reading a univariate forecast as if it knew about covariates. The scope page covers each and the misuse page has worked examples.

Evaluation

Why is likelihood the default score, not CRPS?

A skater's target is a reusable predictive density, and densities are chained: each stage refines the residual of the last. Held-out log-likelihood is the score under which those refinements add up. CRPS suits a different target, coverage or threshold behaviour, and it is reported alongside. The full argument, with a worked example and the cases where CRPS is right, is on the metrics page.

Isn't the benchmark win just p-hacked?

Every comparison is paired per series with a Diebold–Mariano test, on identical windows, and the results include the strata where laplace loses. Against foundational models it is currently a win only on daily economic series. On the others, models such as Timestral and TimeH2O are slightly better, and more will probably have joined them by the time you read this. They also have quite different deployment properties. The leaderboards let you choose the reference model and slice the series by type.

Where can I see how it compares with a particular model?

Each model has its own page on foundationalprediction.com, with how it was run, its references and its paired results on every panel. The interactive leaderboard there lets you make any model the reference.

Why the emphasis on one step ahead and short horizons?

Your criteria may differ, and you can use the components as you wish. The author's uses are largely financial, and there is often a strong reason to focus on k=1. A very brief version of the argument runs as follows. An investor who can trade every interval in a liquid market chooses today's position from the distribution of the next return; under log utility nothing further ahead enters the decision. So one step ahead is the score that matters for that use, and the comparisons here report it on its own rather than pooled over long horizons. The argument, with its limits, is in On the importance of evaluating foundational time-series models one step ahead in an investment context. Multi-step forecasts are supported to twelve steps, which is the stated upper limit.

Doesn't the transform leave the hard part, the time-dependence, exactly as hard? (the copula objection)

No. The copula instinct is right for the static transform: push a series through a fixed marginal F and all the serial dependence is still sitting in the uniforms, exactly as hard as before. This transform is the conditional one. F is a one-step-ahead forecast, refreshed every tick, so the time-dependence the forecaster can model is removed before z exists, and what reaches z is only what it missed.

So the objection is really a claim about the forecaster, and the forecaster earns it. Laplace leads the held-out likelihood race against classical, neural and foundation-model baselines on the non-price universe (the benchmarks), and the transformed streams show what that buys: hand z to ARIMA, ETS, GARCH or Prophet and they find almost nothing left, a few hundredths of a nat between them (results log); hand it to the streaming anomaly detectors in timemachines and they run several times better than on raw data. Most of the dependence problem is finished by the time z exists.

What does remain as hard as before is cross-stream dependence, which a univariate forecaster cannot see; that is second-stage work in z, with log-likelihood adding across the stages. Watch it in the Rosenblatt demo, or read parade.py; the bookkeeping is about twenty lines.

"A nonlinear transform of an optimal estimate is usually not the optimal estimate of the nonlinear transform." Isn't this exactly that mistake?

The rule is E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]), and it bites when a point goes through a nonlinearity. Distributions do not have this problem: if Y has law F then F(Y) is uniform, the pullback of a density is exactly a density, and there is no Jensen gap in the map itself. The only estimated object is F, as in any model.

The rule's stated exception is maximum likelihood, and that exception is the design principle here. Everything is scored by log-likelihood, the identity log p(y) = log f̂(y) + log q(z) − log φ(z) holds for the actual estimated f̂, and estimation error in F therefore shows up as measured miscalibration in the parade rather than hiding inside the nonlinearity. When a point is needed it is the mean of the pulled-back distribution by quadrature, the optimal estimate of the transform.

The codebase contains both versions of the rule: the obedient quadrature, and a point plugged through the inverse map. The plug-in lost, and the results log reports it in a footnote. Move the slider below: the transform of the mean stays at 1 while the mean of the transform does not.

y = exp(σz), z ~ N(0,1)  ·  skew σ

Online methods struggle to adjust parameters without a restart. How is that handled?

There is no fit-then-freeze stage, so there is nothing to restart. Every estimator is recursive with forgetting, the ensemble reweights its candidates every tick, a regime change demotes candidates instead of forcing a refit, and windup guards reset degenerate estimators locally.

Adaptation is monitored rather than hidden: the parade computes prequential calibration each tick, so parameter lag shows up as miscalibration, and memory speed is a knob rather than a buried constant. A structural break still costs something, and the benchmarks measure that cost. Any body can be checkpointed, forked or restarted from its state dict; the reweighting is in terminal.py, and the playground demo lets you switch regimes mid-stream and watch.
