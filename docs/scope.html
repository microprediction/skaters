Scope

What autonomous univariate distributional forecasting is for, and the objections it is not refuted by.

What it is

A skater turns a single numeric stream into a full predictive distribution, online, automatically, in Python or the browser. Not a point and an error bar bolted on afterwards. A distribution: a mean for an estimate, a spread for risk, tails for sizing, and a density for scoring and combination. No human tends any one series, and nothing is configured per series.

What it is good for

A distribution, not a point, is what downstream decisions actually consume. Risk limits read the spread, threshold alarms read the tail probability, position sizing reads the whole shape, simulation reads draws, and a likelihood-based evaluation reads the density at what happened. A skater supplies all of these for any stream it is pointed at.

It is built to run unattended and at scale: thousands of streams, updated on every observation, on a server or in a browser tab, with no fitting job to babysit. That makes it a good baseline, a good prior, and a good monitor — the always-on distributional layer under a system that has better things to spend attention on.

A calibrated surprise signal falls out for free. Because every prediction is a density, every arriving point gets a probability integral transform against the forecast that was made for it: the state carries state["pit"] (roughly Uniform when the stream is behaving) and state["z"] (roughly N(0,1)) at every horizon 1..k, resolved online with no extra compute — scored against a model that has already adapted to the stream's drift, volatility clock, seasonality, and coordinate, which is precisely what separates a genuine surprise from a regime the model should have learned. Turning that signal into streaming anomaly detection with measured false-alarm rates is its own project, timemachines, built on skaters.

The objections it is not refuted by

“But you need more data, or exogenous features.” Yes, and univariate forecasting never claimed otherwise. Real series are driven by outside data, and a serious model brings it in. Univariate distributional prediction is a component, not a pipeline. It is what you run on the series you have, and it plugs in before, during, or after the rest of the modeling. A skater is a transformation by construction, so it composes into a larger system rather than standing alone; exogenous structure enters as another stage that conditions on it and refines the residual.

“You should just hire an analyst, or build a bespoke model.” For one series, sure. The point is autonomy and scale. A hand-built model per stream does not survive thousands of streams updated online, and a point forecast from it is not the object most decisions need. A skater is cheap, fast, dependency-free, and runs anywhere, which is exactly what you want for the long tail of series nobody will ever staff.

Chaining into larger systems

This is the point the Microprediction book is built around. Forecasting can be organized as a supply chain: one stream's distributional output becomes the input, the residual, or the settled outcome of the next, each stage contributed by whoever is best at it. A univariate distributional forecaster is the base layer of that chain, the reusable density that residual markets, dependence models, and conditioning forecasters build on.

That is also why a skater is scored by held-out log-likelihood rather than by a threshold or interval metric. Likelihood is the score under which such refinements compose: the log-likelihood of a chained forecast is the base score plus the contribution of each stage. The reasoning is laid out in why skaters ranks by likelihood.

Replacing running standardization

Full-sample or centered-window standardization can use future observations. A prequential probability integral transform avoids that look-ahead by evaluating each observation under a distribution issued before it arrived. A strictly trailing z-score is already causal: the distributional alternative can represent skew and tail behavior beyond location and scale, and supplies a calibration diagnostic. Both approaches can lag regime changes. For a calibrated, continuous predictive CDF, the ideal PIT is uniform and its inverse-normal transform is standard-normal before numerical clamping (discrete outcomes need a randomized PIT for that guarantee). It is a stateful alternative, with warm-up and additional computation, rather than an unconditional drop-in. The standardization-review skill explains these trade-offs.

Prior knowledge

What follows is obvious. It is written down because it turns out not to be obvious to everyone on LinkedIn who calls themselves a data science leader. The algorithm does not know that you are modelling a non-negative series, if you are. It sees a stream of numbers on the real line and forecasts a distribution on the real line, and nothing in it recognises electricity demand or a hash rate as something that cannot fall below zero.

If you know the series is positive, say so: choose a bijection from the real line to the positive half, run laplace in the transformed coordinate, and map the predictive back. That is what gibrat does with the log map, and it is how every transform in the package works, so adapting the same move to other kinds of prior knowledge (a ceiling, a known periodicity, a known mean to revert to, a known unit of account) is mostly mechanical, and Claude or a competent intern will manage it from the examples. The forecaster is the last link in a chain, and the chain is where what you know goes; see tiki-taka for the passing game.

Units and scale

A skater is not unit-free. Its transforms carry constants in absolute units: a variance intercept, a ridge, floors that stop a division by zero. They were tuned, and granger was trained, on series whose values and typical steps sit roughly between 10-3 and 103. On a test series, laplace gave the same forecast to four figures whether the data were scaled by 10-4 or 108, drifted at 1010 and 10-10, and failed past 1014. A series at 1020 or 10200 is representable in floating point, and the model will still run on it, and get it wrong.

So choose units the way a person would. Hash rate in exahashes per second, not hashes; GDP in billions, not dollars; a quantity that lives at 10-12 in its own scaled unit. Keep values and steps inside about 10-3 to 106 and nothing else needs changing. A series that grows multiplicatively across many orders of magnitude over its life is a different matter: no fixed unit fits all of it, and the log coordinate is the right one (see gibrat on the examples page).

Horizons and sampling rates

The maintainer's own use is one step ahead, and sometimes a few steps, on daily and weekly economic series. That is where laplace has been exercised hardest, and where the benchmarks on this site live. Multi-step forecasting is supported and tested up to k=12, which the author treats as the upper limit and as ongoing work: the multi-scale mixture and the horizon-dependent pieces (tails, coordinate inverses) are still being improved there. Beyond twelve steps, build the stride into the clock (decimate) rather than asking for a longer fan.

Heavily over-sampled data is a different regime: a physiological signal at hundreds of samples a second, a sensor read far faster than anything in it changes, tick data. Successive observations are then nearly deterministic functions of each other, and a one-step predictive is mostly a statement about interpolation. laplace will run, but it may not be the right instrument, and no benchmark here says it is. The better path is to treat the package as a toolkit: pick the transforms that encode what you know about the signal, choose the clock (decimate, or forecast at the stride that matters), and compose a skater for the job from the same parts laplace is built from. The examples page and the methodology guide show how.

The short version

Univariate distributional forecasting is not the whole system. It is the calibrated, autonomous, composable base of one: a full distribution for every stream, at scale, ready to be chained.
